Every derivative formula, read backwards, is an integration formula. With the Fundamental Theorem, these give exact values of definite integrals. Applied to a rate of change, the theorem says something concrete: integrating a rate over an interval gives the net change of the quantity, for example the displacement of a moving object from its velocity.

Learning objectives Use the basic integration formulas; apply the net change theorem; distinguish net displacement from total distance traveled; use symmetry to integrate even and odd functions over [−a, a].

02 · The net change theorem

Net change theorem

This is Part 2 of the Fundamental Theorem applied to a rate: ∫ab F′(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), the net change of F from a to b. Since velocity is v(t) = s′(t),

∫ab v(t) dt = s(b) − s(a) = net displacement,   ∫ab |v(t)| dt = total distance traveled.

Example: a decelerating car has v(t) = −t/5 + 20 m/s. From t = 0 to t = 100 it travels ∫0100 (−t/5 + 20) dt = [−t²/10 + 20t]0100 = −1000 + 2000 = 1000 m.

03 · Even and odd functions

Symmetric intervals

If f is even (f(−x) = f(x)), then ∫−aa f(x) dx = 2∫0a f(x) dx: the two halves are mirror images. If f is odd (f(−x) = −f(x)), then ∫−aa f(x) dx = 0: the area below the axis cancels the area above.
