Because (eˣ)′ = eˣ and (ln|x|)′ = 1/x, exponential functions and the reciprocal function are easy to integrate. Combined with substitution, they handle a large family of integrals: anything of the form eg(x)·g′(x) or g′(x)/g(x).

Each rule can be checked by differentiating; for example (x ln x − x)′ = ln x + x·(1/x) − 1 = ln x.

03 · Integrals that give logarithms

ln x as an area

By the Fundamental Theorem, ln x = ∫1x dt/t for x > 0: the area under y = 1/t from 1 to x (counted negative when x < 1). Properties of ln become facts about areas: for example, the area from a to ab equals the area from 1 to b, which is why ln(ab) = ln a + ln b.

04 · An application: price and demand

Recover a function from its rate

A store finds that the price p (in dollars) at which it can sell x tubes of toothpaste per week changes at the rate p′(x) = −0.015e−0.01x. At a price of $2.35 it sells 50 tubes per week. Integrating, p(x) = ∫ −0.015e−0.01x dx = 1.5e−0.01x + C. From p(50) = 2.35: C = 2.35 − 1.5e−0.5 ≈ 1.44, so p(x) ≈ 1.5e−0.01x + 1.44. To sell 100 tubes per week, the price should be p(100) = 1.5e−1 + 1.44 ≈ $1.99. (Rounding e−0.5 to 0.6 gives C = 1.45 and about $2.00.)
