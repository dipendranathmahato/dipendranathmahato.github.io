Most functions in calculus belong to a few families: lines, polynomials, power and rational functions, roots, and the transcendental functions (trigonometric, exponential, logarithmic). In this section we describe how a function behaves (increasing, decreasing, one-to-one, even or odd), how to combine two functions, and how shifts and stretches move a graph.

Learning objectives Decide where a function increases or decreases; find slopes and equations of lines; test whether a function is one-to-one; combine and compose functions; recognize even and odd functions; identify polynomials, power, algebraic, and transcendental functions; solve quadratic equations; transform graphs.

01 · Increasing, decreasing, and constant

Increasing and decreasing on an interval I
  • f is increasing on I if x₁ < x₂ in I implies f(x₁) ≤ f(x₂), and strictly increasing if it implies f(x₁) < f(x₂).
  • f is decreasing on I if x₁ < x₂ implies f(x₁) ≥ f(x₂), and strictly decreasing if it implies f(x₁) > f(x₂).
  • A constant function f(x) = c has f(x₁) = f(x₂) for all inputs, so it is neither strictly increasing nor strictly decreasing.

02 · Lines and slope

Equations of a line

The line through (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) has slope m = (y₂ − y₁)/(x₂ − x₁).

  • Slope-intercept form: y = mx + b, with y-intercept (0, b).
  • Standard form: ax + by = c.
  • m > 0: increasing; m < 0: decreasing; m = 0: constant (horizontal). A vertical line x = k has undefined slope and is not a function.

03 · One-to-one functions

One-to-one (injective)

f is one-to-one if different inputs always give different outputs: x₁ ≠ x₂ implies f(x₁) ≠ f(x₂). Graphically, every horizontal line meets the graph at most once. Strictly increasing and strictly decreasing functions are one-to-one.

04 · Combining and composing functions

Algebra of functions and composition

(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x), (f − g)(x) = f(x) − g(x), (f · g)(x) = f(x)g(x), and (f/g)(x) = f(x)/g(x) where g(x) ≠ 0.

The composition g ∘ f ("g composed with f") is (g ∘ f)(x) = g(f(x)): f acts first, then g acts on f(x). It is defined when the outputs of f are inputs of g.

BE CAREFUL
  • Order matters: in general (f ∘ g)(x) ≠ (g ∘ f)(x).
  • Composition is not multiplication: (f · g)(x) = f(x)g(x), but (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)).

05 · Even and odd functions

Symmetry
  • f is even if f(−x) = f(x) for all x in the domain: the graph is symmetric about the y-axis.
  • f is odd if f(−x) = −f(x) for all x in the domain: the graph is symmetric about the origin.
  • To test, replace x by −x and simplify. Many functions are neither.

For example, xⁿ is even when n is even and odd when n is odd.

06 · Polynomials and the quadratic formula

Polynomial functions

A polynomial is f(x) = aₙxⁿ + aₙ₋₁xⁿ⁻¹ + ⋯ + a₁x + a₀ with aₙ ≠ 0. Its degree is n and its leading coefficient is aₙ. Degree 2 is quadratic and degree 3 is cubic. For example, x² + 6x + 9 is quadratic with leading coefficient 1, and 100x⁴⁷ + 8 has degree 47 and leading coefficient 100.

The roots of ax² + bx + c = 0 are x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/(2a). The discriminant b² − 4ac decides how many real roots there are.

07 · Power, algebraic, and transcendental functions; transformations

More families
  • Power functions f(x) = axᵇ, such as x⁴, √x = x^(1/2), and 1/x = x⁻¹.
  • Algebraic functions are built from polynomials with +, −, ×, ÷, and roots: rational functions p(x)/q(x) with q(x) ≠ 0, and root functions √(p(x)) with p(x) ≥ 0. For example, (3x − 1)/(5x + 2) has domain x ≠ −2/5 and range y ≠ 3/5, and √(4 − x²) has domain [−2, 2] and range [0, 2].
  • Transcendental functions are not algebraic. The most common are the trigonometric, exponential, and logarithmic functions.
  • The absolute value |x| is −x for x < 0 and x for x ≥ 0.
