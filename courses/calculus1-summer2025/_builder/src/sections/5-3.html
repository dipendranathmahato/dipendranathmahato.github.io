Calculus has two halves: differentiation, the study of tangent lines, and integration, the study of areas under curves. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects them. If we let the right end of an area slide, the area becomes a function of x, and its derivative is the original function. As a consequence, a definite integral can be computed from any antiderivative, with no limits of sums at all.

Learning objectives Describe the accumulation function F(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt; state and use Part 1 of the Fundamental Theorem, including with the chain rule; state and use Part 2 to evaluate definite integrals; recognize when the theorem does not apply; state the Mean Value Theorem for integrals.

01 · The accumulation function

Area as a function of the right endpoint

For fixed a and b, ∫ab f(t) dt is a number. Keep t = a fixed and let the right end be a variable x, like a slider. Then

F(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt

is a function of x, the accumulation function: the net signed area from a to x. F increases where f > 0 (positive area is being added) and decreases where f < 0.

02 · The Fundamental Theorem, Part 1

FTC Part 1

If f is continuous on [a, b] and F(x) = ∫ax f(t) dt, then F is differentiable and

F′(x) = d/dx ∫ax f(t) dt = f(x).

Why: F(x + h) − F(x) = ∫xx+h f(t) dt, the area of a thin strip of width h and height about f(x). So (F(x + h) − F(x))/h ≈ f(x)·h/h = f(x), and the approximation becomes exact as h → 0. In particular, every continuous function has an antiderivative.

With the chain rule: if f is continuous and g, h are differentiable, then d/dx ∫g(x)h(x) f(t) dt = f(h(x))·h′(x) − f(g(x))·g′(x).

03 · The Fundamental Theorem, Part 2

FTC Part 2 (the evaluation theorem)

If f is continuous on [a, b] and F is any antiderivative of f, then

∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a) = F(x)|ab.

The constant C cancels, so any antiderivative works. For example, ∫02 x² dx = x³/3 |02 = 8/3 − 0 = 8/3.

CHECK CONTINUITY FIRST

04 · The Mean Value Theorem for integrals

Mean Value Theorem for integrals

If f is continuous on [a, b], there is at least one c in [a, b] with f(c) = (1/(b − a)) ∫ab f(x) dx: the function actually takes its average value somewhere. For f(x) = x² on [0, 4], the average is 16/3, and c² = 16/3 gives c = 4/√3 ≈ 2.309.
