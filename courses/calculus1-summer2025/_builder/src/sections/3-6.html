We can differentiate sin x and x³, but what about sin(x³)? It is a composition: first cube, then take the sine. The chain rule says that rates of change multiply: if u changes 3x² times as fast as x, and y changes cos u times as fast as u, then y changes cos u · 3x² times as fast as x. At the end of the section we use the same idea to differentiate inverse functions, including the inverse trigonometric functions.

Learning objectives Identify the inside and outside functions of a composition; apply the chain rule in both notations; combine it with the power, trigonometric, product, and quotient rules; use the chain rule with a table of values; differentiate inverse functions and the six inverse trigonometric functions.

01 · The chain rule

Chain rule

If h(x) = (f ∘ g)(x) = f(g(x)), with g differentiable at x and f differentiable at g(x), then

h′(x) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x): (derivative of the outside, evaluated at the inside) × (derivative of the inside).

In Leibniz notation, with u = g(x) and y = f(u): dy/dx = (dy/du) · (du/dx). For example, d/dx sin(x³) = cos(x³) · 3x²; at x = a this is cos(a³) · 3a², exactly what the limit definition gives after multiplying and dividing by x³ − a³.

04 · Derivatives of inverse functions

Inverse function rule

If f is one-to-one and differentiable with f′(f⁻¹(x)) ≠ 0, then differentiating f(f⁻¹(x)) = x with the chain rule gives f′(f⁻¹(x)) · (f⁻¹)′(x) = 1, so

(f⁻¹)′(x) = 1 / f′(f⁻¹(x)).

Geometrically: the graph of f⁻¹ is the mirror image of the graph of f in y = x, so the tangent slopes at mirror points are reciprocals.
